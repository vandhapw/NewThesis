\section{Foundations of Smart Manufacturing and Industry 4.0}

\subsection{Evolution of Manufacturing Paradigms}

The transition from traditional manufacturing to smart manufacturing represents one of the most significant industrial transformations of the twenty-first century \cite{mobley_introduction_2002, jardine_review_2006}. As established in Chapter~1, Industry~4.0 integrates cyber-physical systems, the Industrial Internet of Things, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence to create autonomous, self-optimising production environments. This section reviews the literature spanning the foundational technologies that enable such environments, the anomaly detection and predictive maintenance methods applied within them, the emerging roles of quantum computing and agentic AI, and the comparative state of existing integrated approaches.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/images/industry40_evolution.png}

    \label{fig:industry40_evolution}
\end{figure}

The four industrial revolutions, depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:industry40_evolution}, mark distinct shifts in production philosophy. Industry~1.0 introduced mechanization powered by water and steam, replacing manual craftsmanship with machine-based manufacturing. Industry~2.0 brought mass production through electrification and assembly lines, enabling standardized output at unprecedented scale. Industry~3.0 added automation through programmable logic controllers and computer-aided manufacturing, decoupling production rate from direct human intervention. Industry~4.0 represents the current frontier, where cyber-physical systems, industrial IoT, and artificial intelligence converge to create self-monitoring, self-configuring, and self-optimising factories that respond in real time to operational conditions.

Jain et al. \cite{jain_anomaly_2016} investigate anomaly detection in smart-city wireless sensor networks, demonstrating that spatial correlation between co-located sensors and robust handling of sensor failures versus genuine anomalies provide methodological insights directly transferable to industrial fleet deployments.

Anomaly detection in industrial time-series data has been revolutionised by deep learning architectures. Chalapathy and Chawla \cite{Chalapathy2019} provide the most comprehensive survey of deep anomaly detection, systematically reviewing autoencoders, variational autoencoders, recurrent neural networks, convolutional networks, and generative adversarial networks across application domains spanning cyber-intrusion detection, fraud, medical imaging, and industrial monitoring. Their taxonomy distinguishes unsupervised, semi-supervised, and supervised paradigms, demonstrating that reconstruction-based autoencoders trained exclusively on normal data are among the most effective approaches for industrial scenarios characterised by abundant normal data and rare anomalous events. Pang et al. \cite{pang_deep_2021} extend this foundation with a systematic review of deep learning for anomaly detection, confirming that normal-data-only training yields superior performance in imbalanced industrial settings. The 8.92\% anomaly rate in the research dataset aligns precisely with this scenario.

\subsection{Autoencoder and Memory-Augmented Architectures}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/images/autoencoder_schema.png}
    \caption{Schema of an autoencoder architecture comprising encoder, latent bottleneck, and decoder components. The bottleneck constrains learned representations, enabling reconstruction-based anomaly detection when anomalous inputs deviate from normal prototypes. Image from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).}
    \label{fig:autoencoder_schema}
\end{figure}

Standard autoencoders suffer from a critical weakness: they generalise so effectively during training that anomalous inputs can be reconstructed with low error, undermining the detection principle. Gao et al. \cite{Gao2022} address this limitation through TSMAE, a Memory-Augmented Autoencoder for IoT time-series data that stores prototypical normal patterns in a fixed-size memory module. The decoder reconstructs inputs solely from memory-retrieved representations, ensuring that anomalous inputs which do not match any stored prototype generate high reconstruction errors. This mechanism directly addresses the false-negative problem on the research dataset, where five distinct failure modes create multiple normality-deviation patterns that standard autoencoders might learn to reconstruct. Chen et al. \cite{chen_unsupervised_2020} extend the autoencoder paradigm with sliding-window convolutional variational autoencoders for industrial robots, demonstrating that converting continuous time series into fixed-length segments achieves state-of-the-art detection performance on robotic actuator degradation data.

Industrial machines are monitored through multiple heterogeneous sensors whose joint behaviour encodes richer failure signatures than any single modality. On the research dataset, the correlation matrix reveals that temperature is the dominant anomaly predictor ($r = 0.447$), while disruptions to inter-sensor relationships provide early warning signals for impending failures. These findings provide theoretical justification for the multi-sensor fusion design in QASAMAP's encoding layer.

\subsection{Quantum Superposition and the Bloch Sphere}

Unlike classical bits that occupy exactly one of two states ($0$ or $1$), a quantum bit (qubit) can exist in a coherent superposition of both states simultaneously. The state of a single qubit is represented by a unit vector in a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
    \label{eq:qubit_state}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$ are probability amplitudes. The geometric interpretation of this state is the Bloch sphere, a unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$ where every point on the surface corresponds to a unique pure qubit state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/images/bloch_sphere.svg}
    \caption{The Bloch sphere, the canonical geometric representation of a single-qubit state $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$. The north pole represents $|0\rangle$, the south pole $|1\rangle$, and every other point on the surface a superposition of the two basis states. Image from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).}
    \label{fig:bloch_sphere}
\end{figure}

In the Bloch sphere parameterisation shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bloch_sphere}, the state is written as $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$, where $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ is the polar angle and $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$ is the azimuthal angle. The Pauli-$Z$ operator measures the projection onto the $z$-axis and its expectation value is $\langle Z \rangle = \cos\theta$, yielding $+1$ at the north pole ($|0\rangle$, safe zone) and $-1$ at the south pole ($|1\rangle$, danger zone). This continuous spectrum between $+1$ and $-1$ is the property that QASAMAP's Pauli-Z encoding heuristic mirrors classically: rather than a binary safe/danger indicator, the sensor risk is encoded as a continuous value capturing the degree of deviation from normality.

\subsection{Quantum Entanglement and Multi-Sensor Correlation}

Quantum entanglement is a uniquely quantum mechanical phenomenon in which the state of a composite system cannot be factored into a product of individual subsystem states. For a two-qubit system, the maximally entangled Bell state is:
\begin{equation}
    |\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\right)
    \label{eq:bell_state}
\end{equation}
Measuring the first qubit immediately collapses the second qubit to the same state, regardless of spatial separation. This non-local correlation, which Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen famously critiqued as ``spooky action at a distance,'' has been experimentally verified through Bell inequality tests and underpins quantum communication and quantum error correction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/images/epr_paradox.svg}
    \caption{Illustration of the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) paradox: two entangled particles are prepared in a correlated state and separated; measurement of one particle instantaneously determines the state of the other, demonstrating quantum non-locality. Image from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).}
    \label{fig:epr_paradox}
\end{figure}

In the context of industrial sensor monitoring, entanglement provides a mathematical metaphor for capturing cross-sensor correlations that classical independent-feature models miss. Just as the state of one entangled qubit is inseparable from its partner, the health of one machine sensor may be intrinsically linked to another through shared operational context. The QASAMAP Pauli-Z encoding circuit uses CNOT gates between adjacent sensor qubits to introduce entanglement-like correlations in the joint density matrix, ensuring that the composite risk indicator reflects inter-sensor dependencies rather than treating each sensor in isolation.

\subsection{Variational Quantum Circuits}

Variational Quantum Circuits (VQC), also known as Parameterised Quantum Circuits (PQC), form the algorithmic backbone of near-term quantum machine learning in the NISQ era. A VQC consists of three layers: an encoding layer that maps classical data into quantum states, a variational layer of parameterised unitary operations, and a measurement layer that extracts classical expectations from the resulting quantum state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/images/vqe_illustration.png}
    \caption{High-level illustration of a Variational Quantum Algorithm (VQE). Classical input data are encoded into a parameterised quantum circuit (ansatz); the resulting state is measured to compute an expectation value (e.g., energy); a classical optimiser updates the parameters iteratively to minimise the objective. Image from Wikimedia Commons (CC0).}
    \label{fig:vqe_illustration}
\end{figure}

The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA) are the two most widely applied VQC paradigms. VQE iteratively minimises the expectation value of a Hamiltonian $\langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | H | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle$ using a classical optimiser such as COBYLA or SPSA to update the circuit parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. QAOA, introduced by Farhi et al.~\cite{Farhi2014}, alternates between a problem Hamiltonian $U_C(\gamma) = e^{-i\gamma H_P}$ and a mixing Hamiltonian $U_B(\beta) = e^{-i\beta \sum_i X_i}$ for $p$ layers, starting from an equal superposition $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$. The depth $p$ controls the approximation quality: as $p \to \infty$, QAOA converges to the exact ground state under mild conditions. For manufacturing scheduling, $H_P$ encodes the maintenance conflict graph and machine risk biases, while the mixer $U_B$ enables exploration of the exponentially large assignment space.

Kreplin et al.~\cite{kreplin_squlearn_2025} develop the sQUlearn library for variational quantum machine learning, offering hardware-efficient ansatz designs and automatic gradient computation via the parameter-shift rule. These VQC foundations directly inform QASAMAP's quantum-inspired heuristic layer: although the current implementation executes classical approximations (random local search for QUBO, graph message passing for QAOA), the mathematical structure of the ansatz, Hamiltonian, and expectation measurement is preserved so that the same formulations can be executed on quantum hardware when fault-tolerant processors become available.

\subsection{Quantum-Inspired Classical Optimization}

Quantum-inspired classical optimization refers to the family of heuristic algorithms that replicate the mathematical structure of quantum computational paradigms---such as superposition, entanglement, and Hamiltonian evolution---while executing entirely on classical processors. Rather than simulating full quantum wavefunctions, these heuristics encode problem variables into pseudo-quantum states and apply update rules analogous to quantum gates or annealing schedules to explore the solution landscape. The approach is motivated by the observation that the \emph{mathematical formulation} of quantum algorithms often captures problem structure more naturally than classical gradient descent or greedy search, even when the hardware required to execute the quantum version is not yet available \cite{Blekos2024, Preskill2018}.

In the context of combinatorial maintenance scheduling, three quantum-inspired heuristics are particularly relevant. \textbf{Pauli-Z multi-sensor encoding} treats each machine's normalised sensor vector as a set of rotation angles $\theta_i = \pi \cdot \mathrm{norm}_i$ and computes a composite expectation $\langle Z \rangle_c = \sum_i w_i \cos(\theta_i)$, mimicking the measurement statistics of a parameterised quantum circuit without ever constructing the state vector. This yields a continuous risk spectrum rather than a binary threshold, enabling detection of sub-threshold risk combinations that classical discrete indicators miss. \textbf{QUBO-formulated local search} encodes the maintenance priority problem as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization objective $E(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T Q \mathbf{x}$ and solves it via greedy seeding plus random bit-flip perturbation, preserving the QUBO energy landscape that a quantum annealer would navigate. \textbf{QAOA-formulated graph scheduling} interprets the T-GCN adjacency matrix as a problem Hamiltonian and performs weighted-neighbour averaging to propagate risk influence across the machine graph, analogous to the QAOA mixer Hamiltonian's role in diffusing amplitude between conflicting assignments.

A critical distinction must be maintained between quantum-inspired heuristics and genuine quantum computation. Quantum-inspired algorithms run in polynomial time on classical CPUs and can be analysed with standard complexity theory; they do not enjoy the exponential state-space parallelism of true quantum superposition and are not subject to decoherence or gate-error limitations. However, by preserving the ansatz structure, Hamiltonian formulation, and measurement semantics of their quantum counterparts, they create a direct migration path: when fault-tolerant quantum hardware matures, the same QUBO matrices and QAOA Hamiltonians can be submitted to quantum processors without reformulating the optimization model. This \emph{algorithmic pre-adaptation} strategy is the foundation of QASAMAP's quantum-inspired layer.

\subsection{Limitations of Current Methodologies}

A systematic comparison of state-of-the-art methodologies reveals three persistent gaps that constrain practical deployment. First, the siloed treatment of anomaly detection and prognostics prevents exploitation of shared representations that could improve both tasks simultaneously \cite{kendall_multi-task_2017}. Second, static model architectures cannot adapt to the non-stationary distributions inherent in real industrial operations \cite{lee_autoencoder-based_2024}. Third, the combinatorial complexity of scheduling maintenance across large machine pools exceeds the capabilities of classical greedy heuristics \cite{dalyac_qualifying_2021}. While individual works excel within their respective domains --- TSMAE for memory-augmented anomaly detection \cite{Gao2022}, QAOA for quantum scheduling \cite{rosendo_quantum_2025}, and agentic RAG for knowledge-augmented reasoning \cite{singh_agentic_2025} --- none provides an integrated solution that combines all three capabilities within a unified, adaptive, closed-loop architecture.
