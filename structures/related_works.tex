\section{Foundations of Smart Manufacturing and Industry 4.0}

\subsection{Evolution of Manufacturing Paradigms}

The transition from traditional manufacturing to smart manufacturing represents one of the most significant industrial transformations of the twenty-first century \cite{mobley_introduction_2002, jardine_review_2006}. As established in Chapter~1, Industry~4.0 integrates cyber-physical systems, the Industrial Internet of Things, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence to create autonomous, self-optimizing production environments. This section reviews the literature spanning the foundational technologies that enable such environments, the anomaly detection and predictive maintenance methods applied within them, the emerging roles of quantum computing and agentic AI, and the comparative state of existing integrated approaches.

\section{Anomaly Detection in Manufacturing}

Anomaly detection in industrial time-series data has been revolutionised by deep learning architectures. Chalapathy and Chawla \cite{Chalapathy2019} provide the most comprehensive survey of deep anomaly detection, systematically reviewing autoencoders, variational autoencoders, recurrent neural networks, convolutional networks, and generative adversarial networks across application domains spanning cyber-intrusion detection, fraud, medical imaging, and industrial monitoring. Their taxonomy distinguishes unsupervised, semi-supervised, and supervised paradigms, demonstrating that reconstruction-based autoencoders trained exclusively on normal data are among the most effective approaches for industrial scenarios characterised by abundant normal data and rare anomalous events. Pang et al. \cite{pang_deep_2021} extend this foundation with a systematic review of deep learning for anomaly detection, confirming that normal-data-only training yields superior performance in imbalanced industrial settings. The 8.92\% anomaly rate in the research dataset aligns precisely with this scenario.

\section{Predictive Maintenance Approaches}

Traditional predictive maintenance relies on statistical process control, survival analysis, and physical degradation models. Mobley \cite{mobley_introduction_2002} introduces the foundational concepts of vibration analysis, oil analysis, and thermography for machinery diagnostics. Jardine et al. \cite{jardine_review_2006} provide a comprehensive review of diagnostics and prognostics implementing condition-based maintenance, covering statistical pattern recognition, hidden Markov models, and particle filtering for remaining useful life estimation. While these classical methods offer interpretability and well-understood failure thresholds, they require extensive domain expertise, manual feature engineering, and struggle to model the complex nonlinear relationships between high-dimensional sensor streams and degradation trajectories in modern smart manufacturing environments.

    \centering

    \label{fig:pdm_pipeline}

Deep learning has emerged as the dominant paradigm for data-driven prognostics. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber \cite{Hochreiter1997} introduced long short-term memory networks, which remain the baseline recurrent architecture for temporal degradation modelling. The research dataset's near-uniform RUL distribution (mean 234.27 hours, standard deviation 150.06 hours) confirms that single-snapshot static-feature-based RUL prediction is fundamentally limited, motivating the use of sequential architectures. Ruff et al. \cite{Ruff2021} survey deep one-class classification methods that learn compact descriptions of normality, providing a theoretical framework for understanding how deep networks model the boundary between normal and degraded operational regimes. These works collectively establish that temporal deep learning is essential for capturing the sequential degradation trajectories required for accurate remaining useful life estimation.

Despite substantial progress in both anomaly detection and remaining useful life estimation, the literature predominantly treats these objectives as isolated problems addressed by distinct research communities. Anomaly detection studies focus on identifying outlier patterns \cite{chen_unsupervised_2020}, while prognostics research concentrates on estimating remaining useful life from degradation trajectories \cite{nizam_real-time_2022}, with limited cross-fertilisation between these complementary tasks. Kendall et al. \cite{kendall_multi-task_2017} demonstrated that jointly learned representations can improve individual task performance through uncertainty-weighted loss balancing, yet the predictive maintenance literature has not systematically exploited multi-task principles.

\section{Quantum Computing and Quantum-Inspired AI for Manufacturing}

\subsection{Quantum Superposition and the Bloch Sphere}

Unlike classical bits that occupy exactly one of two states ($0$ or $1$), a quantum bit (qubit) can exist in a coherent superposition of both states simultaneously. The state of a single qubit is represented by a unit vector in a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
    \label{eq:qubit_state}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$ are probability amplitudes. The geometric interpretation of this state is the Bloch sphere, a unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$ where every point on the surface corresponds to a unique pure qubit state.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/images/Bloch_sphere.png}
    \caption{The Bloch sphere, the canonical geometric representation of a single-qubit state $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$. The north pole represents $|0\rangle$, the south pole $|1\rangle$, and every other point on the surface a superposition of the two basis states. Image from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).}
    \label{fig:bloch_sphere}
\end{figure}
In the Bloch sphere parameterization shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bloch_sphere}, the state is written as $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$, where $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ is the polar angle and $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$ is the azimuthal angle. The Pauli-$Z$ operator measures the projection onto the $z$-axis and its expectation value is $\langle Z \rangle = \cos\theta$, yielding $+1$ at the north pole ($|0\rangle$) and $-1$ at the south pole ($|1\rangle$). The continuous spectrum between $+1$ and $-1$ underlies the encoding of binary scheduling decision variables $x_{i,j} \leftrightarrow \tfrac{1}{2}(I - Z_{i,j})$ that QASAMAP's QAOA path uses to map the maintenance assignment problem onto an Ising Hamiltonian (Chapter~6).

\subsection{Quantum Entanglement and Multi-Sensor Correlation}

Quantum entanglement is a uniquely quantum mechanical phenomenon in which the state of a composite system cannot be factored into a product of individual subsystem states. For a two-qubit system, the maximally entangled Bell state is:
\begin{equation}
    |\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\right)
    \label{eq:bell_state}
\end{equation}
Measuring the first qubit immediately collapses the second qubit to the same state, regardless of spatial separation. This non-local correlation, which Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen famously critiqued as spooky action at a distance, has been experimentally verified through Bell inequality tests and underpins quantum communication and quantum error correction.

In QASAMAP, entanglement is exploited operationally rather than metaphorically: the QAOA-XY ring mixer of Chapter~6 entangles the qubits within each machine's $T$-slot block via $\mathrm{CNOT}$--$R_y$--$\mathrm{CNOT}$ patterns so that the joint state of the block remains inside the one-hot subspace under the mixing unitary, structurally preserving the assignment constraint. The cross-machine couplings $J_{ij}$ encoded into the problem Hamiltonian then propagate amplitude across the entangled assignment space during the variational layer.

\subsection{Variational Quantum Circuits}

Variational Quantum Circuits (VQC), also known as Parameterised Quantum Circuits (PQC), form the algorithmic backbone of near-term quantum machine learning in the NISQ era. A VQC consists of three layers: an encoding layer that maps classical data into quantum states, a variational layer of parameterised unitary operations, and a measurement layer that extracts classical expectations from the resulting quantum state.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/images/vqe_illustration.png}
    \caption{High-level illustration of a Variational Quantum Algorithm (VQE). Classical input data are encoded into a parameterised quantum circuit (ansatz); the resulting state is measured to compute an expectation value (e.g., energy); a classical optimiser updates the parameters iteratively to minimise the objective. Image from Wikimedia Commons (CC0).}
    \label{fig:vqe_illustration}
\end{figure}
The operation of a generic VQC can be formalised in three stages. \textbf{Stage 1 --- Encoding.} A classical input vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is mapped into an $n$-qubit quantum state $|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle$ via a data-dependent unitary $U_E(\mathbf{x})$. The most common encoding is angle encoding, where each feature $x_i$ is mapped to a single-qubit rotation angle:
\begin{equation}
    U_E(\mathbf{x}) = \bigotimes_{i=1}^{n} R_Z(x_i) \cdot H^{\otimes n}
    \label{eq:angle_encoding}
\end{equation}
with $H$ the Hadamard gate and $R_Z(\theta) = e^{-i\theta Z/2}$ the Pauli-$Z$ rotation. This creates an entangled superposition state in which the classical information is stored in the relative phases of the computational basis amplitudes.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- Variational Ansatz.} A parameterised unitary $U_V(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is applied to the encoded state. For a hardware-efficient ansatz with $L$ layers, each layer consists of single-qubit rotations followed by two-qubit entangling gates (e.g., CNOT):
\begin{equation}
    U_V(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \Bigl( \bigotimes_{i=1}^{n} R_{P_i}(\theta_{i,\ell}) \Bigr) \cdot U_{\text{ent}}^{(\ell)}
    \label{eq:variational_ansatz}
\end{equation}
where $R_{P_i}$ denotes a rotation around Pauli operator $P_i \in \{X,Y,Z\}$ and $U_{\text{ent}}^{(\ell)}$ is the entangling layer (typically a fixed CNOT ladder or nearest-neighbour pattern). The total number of trainable parameters is $|\boldsymbol{\theta}| = n \cdot L$, scaling linearly with qubit count and circuit depth.

\textbf{Stage 3 --- Measurement and Cost.} The expectation value of an observable $O$ (often a problem Hamiltonian) is estimated by repeated measurement shots:
\begin{equation}
    C(\boldsymbol{\theta}; \mathbf{x}) = \langle \psi(\mathbf{x}) | U_V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, O \, U_V(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | \psi(\mathbf{x}) \rangle
    \label{eq:vqc_cost}
\end{equation}
The cost landscape is non-convex and generally contains barren plateaus at large depth $L$, where gradients vanish exponentially with $n$ \cite{Blekos2024}. A classical optimiser (e.g., COBYLA, SPSA, Adam) updates $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to minimise $C$:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)} - \eta \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} C(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})
    \label{eq:classical_update}
\end{equation}
The gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} C$ cannot be computed classically for a general quantum circuit; instead, the \emph{parameter-shift rule} provides exact gradients from two shifted circuit evaluations:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial C}{\partial \theta_k} = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[ C\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_k\bigr) - C\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_k\bigr) \Bigr]
    \label{eq:parameter_shift}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{e}_k$ is the unit vector along parameter $k$. This rule requires exactly two additional quantum circuit executions per parameter, making gradient-based training feasible on NISQ hardware.

The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA) are the two most widely applied VQC paradigms. VQE iteratively minimises the expectation value of a Hamiltonian $\langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | H | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle$ using a classical optimiser to update $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. QAOA, introduced by Farhi et al.~\cite{Farhi2014}, alternates between a problem Hamiltonian $U_C(\gamma) = e^{-i\gamma H_P}$ and a mixing Hamiltonian $U_B(\beta) = e^{-i\beta \sum_i X_i}$ for $p$ layers, starting from an equal superposition $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$. The depth $p$ controls the approximation quality: as $p \to \infty$, QAOA converges to the exact ground state under mild conditions. For manufacturing scheduling, $H_P$ encodes the maintenance conflict graph and machine risk biases, while the mixer $U_B$ enables exploration of the exponentially large assignment space.

Kreplin et al.~\cite{kreplin_squlearn_2025} develop the sQUlearn library for variational quantum machine learning, offering hardware-efficient ansatz designs and automatic gradient computation via the parameter-shift rule. These VQC foundations directly inform QASAMAP's gate-based scheduling path of Chapter~6, which constructs a parameterised QAOA circuit with the XY ring mixer of Hadfield et al.\ \cite{Hadfield2019} and executes it on the IBM Heron~r2 processor (\texttt{ibm\_fez}) via Qiskit Runtime. Variational parameters $(\gamma, \beta)$ are optimised on a classically simulated reduced-size sub-problem and transferred to the full-size hardware circuit, following the parameter-concentration argument of Brandao et al.\ \cite{Brandao2018}.

\subsection{Quantum Optimization Foundations}

Quantum computing represents not merely an incremental improvement over classical computation but a fundamentally new computational paradigm requiring algorithmic design from first principles \cite{eldh_quantum_2025}. Preskill \cite{Preskill2018} introduced the NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) era concept, characterising current quantum processors with 50--1,000 qubits and finite gate fidelities. Blekos et al. \cite{Blekos2024} systematically analyse the complexity classes for which quantum optimization advantages are theoretically supported, identifying the barren plateau problem as the primary training challenge for variational quantum algorithms and reviewing mitigation strategies including layerwise training and warm-starting from classical solutions.

\textbf{Hamiltonian formulation.} In quantum mechanics, a Hamiltonian $H$ is a Hermitian operator that represents the total energy of a system. Its eigenvalues correspond to the allowed energy levels, and its eigenvectors (eigenstates) describe the stable configurations of the system. For an $n$-qubit system, $H$ is expressed as a sum of Pauli tensor products:
\begin{equation}
    H = \sum_{k} h_k \, P_{k,1} \otimes P_{k,2} \otimes \cdots \otimes P_{k,n}, \qquad P_{k,i} \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}
    \label{eq:hamiltonian_pauli}
\end{equation}
where $h_k \in \mathbb{R}$ are coefficients and $P_{k,i}$ are Pauli operators acting on qubit $i$. The ground state $|\psi_0\rangle$ is the eigenvector with the smallest eigenvalue $E_0$:
\begin{equation}
    H |\psi_0\rangle = E_0 |\psi_0\rangle, \qquad E_0 = \min_{|\psi\rangle} \langle \psi | H | \psi \rangle
    \label{eq:ground_state}
\end{equation}
In combinatorial optimization, the problem is encoded into $H$ such that the ground state corresponds to the optimal solution. For example, a binary variable $x_i \in \{0,1\}$ is mapped to the Pauli-$Z$ operator $Z_i$ via the substitution:
\begin{equation}
    x_i \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{2}(I - Z_i)
    \label{eq:z_substitution}
\end{equation}
since $Z_i$ has eigenvalues $+1$ for $|0\rangle$ and $-1$ for $|1\rangle$, so $\frac{1}{2}(I-Z_i)$ yields eigenvalue $0$ for $|0\rangle$ and $1$ for $|1\rangle$.

\textbf{QUBO and Ising mapping.} A Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem seeks a binary vector $\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^n$ that minimises a quadratic objective:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{QUBO}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Q_{ii} x_i + \sum_{i < j} Q_{ij} x_i x_j = \mathbf{x}^T Q \, \mathbf{x}
    \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is a symmetric matrix encoding linear (diagonal) and quadratic (off-diagonal) coefficients. The QUBO formulation is completely general: any NP-hard combinatorial problem---including MaxCut, graph colouring, travelling salesman, and scheduling---can be polynomially reduced to QUBO \cite{dalyac_qualifying_2021}.

The QUBO objective maps directly to an Ising Hamiltonian through the change of variables $s_i = 2x_i - 1 \in \{-1,+1\}$:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\text{Ising}} = \sum_{i} J_{ii} s_i + \sum_{i < j} J_{ij} s_i s_j + \text{const.}, \qquad J_{ij} = \frac{Q_{ij}}{4}
    \label{eq:ising}
\end{equation}
Quantum annealing hardware (e.g., D-Wave) physically implements $H_{\text{Ising}}$ by encoding $s_i$ as the $z$-component of a superconducting qubit and adjusting the coupler strengths $J_{ij}$ between qubits. The system evolves under a time-dependent Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}
    H(t) = A(t) \sum_i \sigma_i^x + B(t) \left( \sum_i J_{ii} \sigma_i^z + \sum_{i < j} J_{ij} \sigma_i^z \sigma_j^z \right)
    \label{eq:annealing_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $A(t)$ starts large and decreases to zero, while $B(t)$ starts at zero and increases. The system begins in the ground state of the transverse-field term $\sum_i \sigma_i^x$ (a uniform superposition) and adiabatically transitions to the ground state of the Ising term, which encodes the optimal QUBO solution \cite{Preskill2018}.

These theoretical foundations underpin QASAMAP's two scheduling paths of Chapter~6: the classical QUBO encoding consumed by simulated quantum annealing and quantum-inspired evolutionary search, and the gate-based QAOA path with the XY ring mixer that runs on actual IBM Quantum hardware.

\subsection{Hybrid Quantum-Classical Systems}

Hybrid quantum-classical architectures combine quantum subroutines with classical preprocessing and postprocessing to mitigate NISQ-era limitations. Kreplin et al. \cite{kreplin_squlearn_2025} develop the sQUlearn library for quantum machine learning, offering hardware-efficient ansatz designs and automatic gradient computation via the parameter-shift rule. Nagy et al. \cite{nagy_hybrid_2025} demonstrate that encoding classical observations in quantum latent spaces enables reinforcement learning agents to discover policies that classical agents cannot find within practical training budgets. Rodr\'iguez-Soriano et al. \cite{rodriguez-soriano_predicting_2025} address temporal variability in quantum processor error rates, motivating adaptive scheduling strategies that defer quantum computation to low-noise time windows. Epping et al. \cite{Epping2025} provide a systematic introduction to quantum software development and error mitigation. These hybrid paradigms directly inform QASAMAP's scheduling layer of Chapter~6, which combines a classical MILP/CBC reference solver, the quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithm and simulated quantum annealing on the QUBO formulation, and the gate-based QAOA-XY path that submits the parameterised circuit to IBM Heron~r2 via Qiskit Runtime with classical parameter pre-optimisation.

\subsection{Quantum-Inspired Classical Optimization}

Quantum-inspired classical optimization refers to the family of heuristic algorithms that replicate the mathematical structure of quantum computational paradigms---such as superposition, entanglement, and Hamiltonian evolution---while executing entirely on classical processors. Rather than simulating full quantum wavefunctions, these heuristics encode problem variables into pseudo-quantum states and apply update rules analogous to quantum gates or annealing schedules to explore the solution landscape. The approach is motivated by the observation that the \emph{mathematical formulation} of quantum algorithms often captures problem structure more naturally than classical gradient descent or greedy search, even when the hardware required to execute the quantum version is not yet available \cite{Blekos2024, Preskill2018}.

In the context of combinatorial maintenance scheduling, three quantum-inspired families are particularly relevant and each is implemented in QASAMAP's Chapter~6 scheduling layer. \textbf{Quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithms} (QIEA) of Han and Kim \cite{HanKim2002} represent each candidate solution by quantum probability amplitudes $|\psi_i\rangle = \alpha_i|0\rangle + \beta_i|1\rangle$ and update the population via rotation gates rather than classical crossover/mutation; QIEA is run on the QUBO of Chapter~6 with adaptive population and generation counts. \textbf{Path-integral simulated quantum annealing} (SQA) of Kadowaki and Nishimori \cite{KadowakiNishimori1998} maps the quantum transverse-field Ising system to $M$ classical Trotter replicas coupled by an inter-replica term, anneals the transverse field $\Gamma \to 0$, and produces a classical estimate of the quantum-annealed ground state. \textbf{Simulated bifurcation} of Goto et al.\ \cite{Goto2019} mimics the dynamics of Kerr-nonlinear parametric oscillators near a bifurcation point and reaches near-optimal solutions in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ integration steps. All three are classical algorithms that preserve the QUBO/Ising mathematical structure of the problem and can be executed within the 60-s real-time Kafka cycle of QASAMAP's multi-agent layer.

A critical distinction must be maintained between quantum-inspired heuristics and genuine quantum computation. The three families above run in polynomial time on classical CPUs and do not enjoy the exponential state-space parallelism of true quantum superposition, but they share the QUBO/Ising mathematical formulation with their quantum-hardware counterpart. In QASAMAP this is not a substitute for hardware execution: the same QUBO instances are also submitted to actual IBM Heron~r2 hardware via the QAOA-XY path of Chapter~6, allowing direct measurement of where each paradigm wins on streaming-derived instances rather than relying on speculative migration arguments.

\subsection{Closing the Gap: Unified Frameworks}

The most significant limitation of existing approaches is the absence of integrated frameworks that simultaneously achieve multi-sensor anomaly detection, remaining useful life prediction, maintenance scheduling optimization, and autonomous agentic decision-making within a single closed-loop architecture. Current works address one or two of these objectives in isolation: anomaly detection literature \cite{Chalapathy2019, pang_deep_2021} treats detection as a terminal task rather than the first stage of a full predictive maintenance workflow; prognostics research \cite{nizam_real-time_2022} estimates RUL without linking predictions to actionable scheduling decisions; quantum optimization studies \cite{dalyac_qualifying_2021, Blekos2024} formulate abstract scheduling problems without grounding them in real sensor data or agentic action execution; and agentic AI works \cite{erukude_agentic_2025, dwivedi_agentic_2026} reason about decisions without integrating physics-informed quantum heuristics for combinatorial optimization. QASAMAP addresses this fragmentation by unifying spatiotemporal forecasting, multi-task supervised learning, quantum-inspired classical heuristics, and multi-agent LLM orchestration within a single architecture that autonomously adapts to evolving industrial conditions.

\subsection{Limitations of Current Methodologies}

A systematic comparison of state-of-the-art methodologies reveals three persistent gaps that constrain practical deployment. First, the siloed treatment of anomaly detection and prognostics prevents exploitation of shared representations that could improve both tasks simultaneously \cite{kendall_multi-task_2017}. Second, static model architectures cannot adapt to the non-stationary distributions inherent in real industrial operations \cite{lee_autoencoder-based_2024}. Third, the combinatorial complexity of scheduling maintenance across large machine pools exceeds the capabilities of classical greedy heuristics \cite{dalyac_qualifying_2021}. While individual works excel within their respective domains --- TSMAE for memory-augmented anomaly detection \cite{Gao2022}, QAOA for quantum scheduling \cite{rosendo_quantum_2025}, and agentic RAG for knowledge-augmented reasoning \cite{singh_agentic_2025} --- none provides an integrated solution that combines all three capabilities within a unified, adaptive, closed-loop architecture.
